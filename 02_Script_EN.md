## PART 1 — THE PROBLEM (0:00 – 2:35)

### 01 · Title ⏱ 0:20

Good afternoon. My name is Jin Choi. I work between furniture, people and space.
Today: two works and one argument — **that furniture is the smallest unit connecting a person to a space** — and that this matters more now than it used to.

### 02 · The paradox ⏱ 1:00

Let me start with a contradiction. `[pause]`
In the OECD Better Life Index, Korea ranks **first of thirty-eight countries** on housing. We build the best housing in the developed world.
In the same index, on *"do you have someone to rely on in a crisis"*, Korea ranks **thirty-eighth of thirty-eight.** Last. `[pause]`
We live in the best-built homes on earth and we are the loneliest people inside them.
This project lives in that gap.

### 03 · 36.1% ⏱ 1:30

Here is the scale. Eight million forty-five thousand Korean households are one person living alone — **thirty-six point one percent** of every household in the country. Past eight million for the first time in 2024. `[pause]`
This is not a minority story. **This is the standard Korean household.**

### 04 · Loneliness ⏱ 2:00

Living alone became ordinary, and loneliness became ordinary with it.
Sixty-two percent of Seoul's single-person households have experienced loneliness. Thirteen point six percent are socially isolated — meaning there is no one to call when something goes wrong.
And people who feel lonely are twice as likely to experience depression.

### 05 · 15.8 hours ⏱ 2:35

And we are inside more than ever. As of 2022, Seoul residents spend **fifteen point eight hours** a day at home — sixty-six percent of the day. In 2004 it was fourteen point eight. `[pause]`
✂︎ Over the same period, eighty-three percent of young households rent. Eight percent live below the minimum housing standard, double the national rate. Five percent live in spaces that were never built to be lived in.
**Loneliness is a matter between people. But the place where those hours are spent is the room.**

## PART 2 — THE POSITION (2:35 – 5:10)

### 06 · House and room ⏱ 3:05

So here is how I read it. `[pause]`
**The house got better. The room did not.**
We grade a home on three things: how many square metres, how safe, how much it costs. Nobody asks what the space actually does for the person inside it.

### 07 · Why furniture ⏱ 3:55

Why furniture and not architecture? Three reasons.
**One.** In architecture, furniture is the smallest act that defines how a body relates to a space. Change the furniture and you change the relationship — without touching the building.
**Two.** Eighty-three percent of young households cannot modify a wall, a window or a doorway. But the furniture is theirs. **It is the only architecture a tenant owns.**
**Three.** The contact already exists. We hang coats on furniture, sit on it, walk past it every day. The furniture simply does not respond. `[pause]` A response can be designed.
**So the thing to change is not the building. It is the furniture standing in it.**

### 08 · Hypothesis ⏱ 4:30

My hypothesis: **if furniture responds to a person, the space begins to acknowledge that person.**
Two mechanisms. **Arrival** — something changes for no reason other than that I came in. **Presence** — the space remembers how long I stayed. `[pause]`
Two mechanisms. Two works.

### 09 · The system ⏱ 5:10

Together they form one route: from the moment of arrival to the body at rest.
**Outside** is light, weather, sound and other people — everything the room has been shutting out.
**Entrance** is *Warm Welcome*, **dwelling** is *Yellow Box*. `[pause]`
**The two works take the two moments between coming home and leaving again: stepping through the door, and the time you spend sitting.**

## PART 3 — MATERIAL AND MEDIUM (5:10 – 6:50)
> **⚠️ These two slides are the spine of the talk — they answer "why this medium" for a digital media department.**

### 10 · Material — cold ⏱ 5:55

Before the works themselves, two decisions that run through both.
The first is material. `[pause]` **These objects are cold by intent.**
Steel, acrylic, aluminium. Not one warm material in either work — no wood, no fabric.
There is a reason. **Wood or fabric would have made the furniture look warm from the start.** Then any warmth you feel when the light comes on could not be told apart from the warmth of the material. `[pause]`
So the baseline had to be completely cold. The surfaces are achromatic, and nothing looks comfortable until someone touches it. The only warm thing in either work is the light, and a person always causes that light.
**Warmth does not come from the material. It is evidence that a person was here.** `[pause]`
✂︎ And these are the real materials of a Korean rented room — aluminium sash, acrylic, painted steel. Rather than replacing them, I added one condition to them.

### 11 · Why digital media ⏱ 6:50

The second decision is the medium. Why digital media and not just furniture? Four answers.
**One — time becomes a material.** To hold light for exactly as long as someone sat, the furniture has to know a person sat down, measure that duration, store it, and play it back. An inert object cannot hold time. Computation is what makes time a material.
**Two — the furniture becomes the interface.** No button, no app, no screen. A switch has to be pressed deliberately; **load sensing just reads an action you were already doing.** There is nothing to learn, and that is the point.
**Three — causality becomes evidence.** The counter displays the calculation itself. The moment the number is visible, this stops being a nice light and becomes a relationship between me and the space.
**Four — the work becomes an instrument.** A wooden stool leaves no record of who sat on it; this one does. `[pause]`
So: **digital media here is not a medium of expression. It is the minimum condition for treating time as a material.**

## PART 4 — THE WORKS (6:50 – 10:15)

### 12 · Warm Welcome (video) ⏱ 7:05

Work one. *Warm Welcome.*
Steel frame, frosted acrylic, backlight, load sensing. Eighteen hundred millimetres tall.
*(Let the loop run once before speaking again.)*

### 13 · Warm Welcome (mechanism) ⏱ 7:50

A coat rack you keep near the entrance. You come home and hang the bag and jacket you were already carrying. That weight closes a circuit and light comes on from behind. `[pause]`
**You leave the public version of yourself hanging here, and step into your own space.**
Two decisions here.
**It is a coat rack as tall as a person.** Not something you hang a coat on — it becomes the door into your own space.
And the hanging garment becomes a silhouette — the shape of your own body, standing in light.

### 14 · Yellow Box (video) ⏱ 8:05

Work two. *Yellow Box.*
Frosted acrylic on three faces, internal light, seat occupancy sensing, and a counter on the back. **A stool that keeps time.**

### 15 · Yellow Box (mechanism) ⏱ 8:55

**Sit, and it lights. Stand up, and it stays lit for as long as you sat.** `[pause]`
It does not switch off when you leave. It stays lit for exactly as long as you sat, and then it fades. Ten minutes of sitting leaves ten minutes.
The idea is that a room is finished by whoever was last inside it — and it stays finished for a while after they leave.
The afterglow is not a delay. It is a **trace**, proportional to exactly how long a person stayed. `[pause]`
In architecture, the chair defines the smallest relationship between a body and a space. This one records it.

### 16 · The counter ⏱ 9:35

Now the back. `[pause]`
The counter on the rear face counts up while you sit and counts down once you leave.
**The front is what you feel. The back is why.**
This matters more than it looks. Without the number, it reads as a mood lamp or a light that takes a while to switch off. With the number, you can recognise that this light exists because of the time you spent sitting.
✂︎ And the number does one more thing in an exhibition: every visitor who sits down leaves one dwell-time record behind. No questionnaire, no prompting.

### 17 · Technical stack ⏱ 10:15

**Here is how the two works operate.**
*Warm Welcome* senses load at the hanging bar, and once it passes a threshold it holds that state while the load is there and sustains a backlight.
*Yellow Box* senses occupancy, measures and stores the duration, replays it one-to-one and then fades — while displaying the figure and writing a dwell record.
`[pause]`
Two notes. **First, why sense load.** A switch or a motion detector would have worked. Load was chosen because it reads the weight of something actually entrusted to the furniture, not an intention to operate it. Hanging a coat and sitting down become the signal as they are.
**Second, why no screen.** A smart home asks you to install an app and look at a display. This furniture asks for nothing. **It only gets used daily if there is nothing to learn.**

## PART 5 — LIMITS AND VISION (10:15 – 12:55)

### 18 · One grammar ⏱ 10:45

Side by side: two works, two relations, two triggers, two responses.
But one rule underneath all of it. **A space must respond to the person inside it.**
That is the grammar, and the two works are two sentences written in it.

### 19 · Limits ⏱ 11:30

Before I go further, let me say what this is not. `[pause]`
A stool does not solve loneliness or isolation. Those are serious social problems, and furniture does not reach that far. These works are a hypothesis about how people perceive space, not welfare policy. I measured no physiological effect and no long-term effect.
What I do claim is narrower. `[pause]`
**Furniture can respond to a person, and that response is something you design.** You decide what it responds to, how it responds and how long it lasts — which also means you can check whether it works. And **you cannot change the building, but you can change the furniture** — it reaches into the room at a cost and scale architecture cannot.

### 20 · DDP ⏱ 11:50

In November the two works go into DDP in the order entrance, then dwelling. `[pause]`
Visitors physically walk the route I have just described. What I want to find out there is one thing: without reading any text, does a visitor realise the light came on because of them?

### 21 · Roadmap ⏱ 12:30

Last thing. `[pause]` These two works are the first two phrases.
**Object** is where I am now. **Set** is next year — furniture responding to furniture; the hanger knows whether the stool is occupied. **Room** is a whole one-person dwelling designed as a responsive space, piloted with housing operators. **Space** is the expansion into total space design: hotels, retail, public interiors. `[pause]`
One principle runs from beginning to end. A space should respond to the person inside it. A stool or a building — it makes no difference.

### 22 · Closing ⏱ 12:55

**A space is finished by whoever was last inside it.** `[pause]`
Eight million people in Korea live alone. The rooms they come home to do nothing at all.
The furniture is already standing there. It only has to respond. `[pause]`
Thank you.

## Anticipated questions

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| **"Why digital media? Isn't this just a lamp?"** | Go back to slide 11. In one line: **an inert object cannot hold duration.** Holding light for exactly as long as someone sat is impossible without computation. Digital media here is not expression; it is the minimum condition for treating time as a material. |
| **"Why load sensing? Why not a button?"** | A button requires an *intention to operate*. Load sensing reads *an action already performed*. Zero learning cost is the design goal, so the sensing method is itself the argument. Motion detection reads passing by, not entrusting. |
| **"What are the exact components?"** | Answer at the level of sensing method: load sensing, occupancy sensing, microcontroller, dimmed output, numeric display, local logging. Specific parts are being finalised in fabrication. |
| **"Aren't cold materials unwelcoming?"** | That is the point. The baseline must be cold for warmth to be attributable to a person. Warm materials make warmth a property of the material. And these are the real materials of a rented room. |
| **"Can furniture solve loneliness?"** | Remind them slide 19 came first. I do not claim it solves anything. I claim responsiveness is a designable variable that is currently neglected. |
| **"Isn't this already in smart homes?"** | Smart-home responses are functional — temperature, security, convenience — and require apps and screens. These works respond only to actions you were already performing, and demand nothing. |
| **"Doesn't it feel like surveillance?"** | Whether it feels welcoming or like being watched is exactly what I will ask visitors at DDP. By design, the data stays local and carries no identifying information. |
| **"Only two works — is that enough?"** | A third work (*Soft Boundary*) was triggered by nature rather than by a person, so it sat on a different axis. Since the argument is that furniture responds to a person, I narrowed it to the two works that sit exactly on that axis. Less breadth, more precise verification of each specification. |
| **"What will the exhibition actually tell you?"** | Three streams: the automatic dwell log, an exit survey and timed observation. The core measure is the difference in comprehension between visitors who saw the rear counter and those who did not — the one question that splits into comparison groups naturally, and the finding I expect to carry the thesis. |
